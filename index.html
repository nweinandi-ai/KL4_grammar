Will-Future

Subject + will + infinitive

Use will for:

use Spontaneous decisions made at the moment of speaking
e.g. "It's cold." — "I'll close the window."
use Predictions based on opinion or belief
e.g. "I think it will rain tomorrow."
use Offers, promises, threats
e.g. "I'll help you with that."

Going-to-Future

Subject + am/is/are + going to + infinitive

Use going to for:

use Plans and intentions already decided before speaking
e.g. "We are going to visit Rome next summer."
use Predictions based on visible evidence right now
e.g. "Look at those clouds — it's going to rain."

Quick comparison

Will

Spontaneous  ·  Promise  ·  Opinion

"I'll call you later." (just decided)

Going to

Pre-planned  ·  Evidence-based

"She's going to call you — she told me."

Key signal words: tomorrow, next week/year, in 2030, soon, I think, I'm sure, I promise, Look! Watch out!

Type 1 — Real / possible condition

If + Simple Present  ,  will + infinitive
use Real, possible situations — the condition can actually happen
e.g. If it rains, we will stay at home.
e.g. If you study hard, you will pass the exam.

Type 2 — Unreal / hypothetical condition (present or future)

If + Simple Past  ,  would + infinitive
use Imaginary or unlikely situations in the present or future
e.g. If I had a million euros, I would travel the world.
e.g. If she were taller, she would be a model.
was or were?
In Type 2 conditionals, the verb to be is special. In formal and written English — and in exams — you should always use were for all persons, including I, he, she, and it:

If I were you, I would apologise. ✓
If he were taller, he could reach it. ✓
If she were here, she would know what to do. ✓

Using was (e.g. "If I was you…") is common in spoken everyday English, but were is the grammatically correct and preferred form in writing and school contexts. The fixed phrase "If I were you…" is always correct and very frequently used.

Type 3 — Unreal condition in the past

If + Past Perfect  ,  would have + past participle
use Imaginary situations in the past — things that did NOT happen
e.g. If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.
e.g. If she had taken the train, she wouldn't have been late.

Active vs. Passive

In an active sentence, the subject does the action.
In a passive sentence, the subject receives the action.

active The dog bites the man. — subject = dog (does the action)
passive The man is bitten by the dog. — subject = man (receives the action)

Use the passive when the action matters more than who does it, or when the doer is unknown or unimportant.

Passive forms — overview

TensePassive formExample
Simple Presentam/is/are + past participleThe letter is written.
Simple Pastwas/were + past participleThe letter was written.
Present Perfecthas/have been + past participleThe letter has been written.
Present Progressiveam/is/are being + past participleThe letter is being written.
Will-Futurewill be + past participleThe letter will be written.
Going-to-Futuream/is/are going to be + past participleThe letter is going to be written.

How to transform: active → passive

1. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject.
2. The verb becomes be + past participle (in the same tense as before).
3. The original subject becomes a by-agent (or is left out if unknown).

Active: Shakespeare wrote Hamlet.
Passive: Hamlet was written by Shakespeare.
